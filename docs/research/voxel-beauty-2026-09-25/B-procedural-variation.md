# B — Procedural variation: how generated places avoid looking mechanical

Research for AI City. Focus: problems 2, 3, 5, 6 from the shared diagnosis (strict-grid repetition, tower window noise, empty flat areas, generic landmarks). Read `original-vs-astra-2026-09-25.jpg` and grepped `world/{districts,identity,outskirts}/*` before writing.

## 1. TL;DR

- Mechanical reads come from the same short list every source names: uniform module reuse, grid-locked placement with no jitter, no size/detail hierarchy, no visible history. Astra's build has versions of all four, in different subsystems.
- AI City's own numbers show it precisely: the low-rise family system draws **216 background buildings from 3 visual families** (brick/pastel/works) — one family repeats ~72 times. The borough system's 5 business-mix tables are each only **6 slots long**, cycled by a fixed per-block offset across 348 buildings, so the same storefront names recur every 6 lots (`districts/plan.mjs`, `identity/borough-plan.mjs`, `identity/CITYWIDE.md`).
- Every "handmade-looking" generator here does the same move: break the module into small interchangeable pieces and let *local adjacency* rules recombine them, instead of picking one whole prebuilt object per lot — Townscaper, Tiny Glade, WFC are all variations on this.
- Variety is always budgeted, never infinite, concentrated where the camera looks. Insomniac's Manhattan (verified, GDC 2019 slides) ships **>8,300 buildings from >3,250 edifice prefabs (~1 per 2.5 buildings)**, with only **~350 storefronts (≈4%)** hand-detailed over procedural fill.
- NYC block rhythm is real historical noise, not decoration: surveyed Manhattan lots run roughly **16–31 ft wide on a single block**, though "25 ft" is the textbook number. That width jitter — not paint — reads as "human scale" from above.
- Rooftop water tanks are a *threshold* detail: NYC requires them above roughly six stories (gravity pressure runs out there). One on every 3–4 story walk-up, as the brief suspected, is itself a tell.
- Fire escapes, dumbbell massing and "Old Law" ornament trace to specific 1867/1879/1901 tenement laws — era-correct assignment, not "fire escape on everything," is a cheap authenticity signal.
- Manhattan barely has alleys; mid-block service happens through basements and narrow private cuts, not a back lane — AI City's empty dock/mall lots need scatter and anchor objects, not alleys.
- Landmarks work by *breaking* the surrounding grammar rarely, not by being a bigger version of the same module. AI City's own original map is the reference: roughly 4–6 landmarks citywide, not one per district.
- The cheapest high-value fix set needs no new geometry budget — mostly widening tables that already exist (6→14+ slots), shuffled draws instead of sequential cycling, and noise moved from per-object to per-band.

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### 2.1 Diagnosis: what makes generated places read as mechanical

No single canonical talk names "the four tells," but the pattern is consistent across every source touched in this research, plus the screenshot comparison itself: **perfect grids**, **repeated modules**, **uniform height and setback** (no skyline incident), **no hierarchy** (nothing bigger or stranger than its neighbors), and **no history** (no wear, no non-conforming infill). A SUPERJUMP essay frames the mechanism well: pure algorithmic noise is "statistically varied but emotionally flat" — the small *quirks* a brain uses to form distinct memories of places are exactly what templated repetition smooths away; hand-made work keeps "individual quirks that reveal the considered hand of a conscious creator" [anecdotal, https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/by-design-procedural-generation/]. This matches the screenshot diagnosis line for line: Astra's suburbs are a perfect grid of one house shape; the towers are one slab shape with per-window noise instead of per-*building* variety; the docks/mall are large uniform flat planes; the courthouse and mall lost their one-off silhouettes.

### 2.2 Techniques from the field

**Townscaper** (Oskar Stålberg, Raw Fury) builds its irregular grid from a hex-triangle base, randomly merges adjacent triangle pairs, converts the result to quads (Conway "ortho" operator), then relaxes across the *infinite* tiling (not per-chunk, so chunks still join seamlessly), "encouraging squarish cell shapes" without forcing a uniform grid [verified, https://boristhebrave.com/docs/sylves/1/articles/tutorials/townscaper.html]. Buildings aren't whole prefabs: a marching-cubes-style pass places small *corner segments* against the irregular topology, and a WFC-derived constraint solve — reused from Stålberg's earlier, rigid-grid game **Bad North** [likely, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_North] — re-evaluates neighbors live. Critically, it uses **hard constraints that forbid identical or mirrored tiles next to each other**, the single most transferable idea here: never let two neighbors read as copies. It's also allowed to *silently fail* on hard cases (long thin steel supports) rather than force an ugly resolution — the failure becomes an acceptable imperfection [verified, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-townscaper-works-a-story-four-games-in-the-making]. The Bad North→Townscaper progression — same author, same algorithm family, only the grid regularity changed — is itself evidence for how much of "mechanical" comes from grid regularity specifically, independent of module quality.

**Tiny Glade** (Anastasia Opara & Tomasz Stachowiak) takes the opposite ratio of automation to authorship: "I create every single rule that goes into the system... everything was hand-crafted by Tom and me," running "a lot of algorithms and generators, each tailored to a specific element" rather than one universal system [verified (thin), https://80.lv/articles/exclusive-tiny-glade-developers-discuss-bevy-proceduralism-publishers-cozy-games]. The transferable lesson isn't a specific grid trick — it's that "procedural" here means *many small, narrowly-scoped, hand-tuned generators*, not one big one.

**Wave Function Collapse** (Maxim Gumin, 2016) generalizes Paul Merrell's **model synthesis** (2007 i3D paper, 2009 PhD dissertation) to overlapping NxN patterns from one example image: the output "should contain only NxN patterns present in the input," collapsed cell-by-cell in minimum-entropy order with constraint propagation to neighbors [verified — Gumin's own README, https://github.com/mxgmn/WaveFunctionCollapse; verified — Merrell's own site, https://paulmerrell.org/model-synthesis/]. This is the formal version of "local coherence, not global template": every local neighborhood has to look like something in the example, but the global arrangement is new every time.

**CGA shape grammar** (Müller, Wonka, Haegler, Ulmer, Van Gool, SIGGRAPH 2006), the academic root of CityEngine, uses **context-sensitive** rules — output depends on a shape's position in a hierarchy and its neighbors, not a fixed lookup — to solve "consistent mass modeling with volumetric shapes of arbitrary orientation" [verified, https://history.siggraph.org/learning/procedural-modeling-of-buildings-by-muller-wonka-haegler-ulmer-and-gool/]. **Parish & Müller 2001** is the street-network ancestor: an L-system grows roads from a priority queue, steered by population-density maps and local junction rules, with buildings placed by nudging a box away from overlaps until it clears or is discarded [likely, https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/1185657.1185716]. **Introversion's Subversion** prototype implemented this method directly and produced a startlingly detailed procedural city down to individual floors — then was shelved in 2011 because the team lost the gameplay underneath a generator that kept getting better [likely, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subversion_(video_game), https://pcg.wikidot.com/pcg-games:subversion]. Main cautionary case study — see §4.

**Insomniac's Manhattan** for *Marvel's Spider-Man* is the strongest primary source here — read directly from David Santiago's GDC 2019 slide deck (89 slides, pages 1–40 reviewed). Verified: **6 km × 3 km, 9 districts, 544 roads, 1,202 alleys, >8,300 buildings, >3,250 edifice prefabs, >350 storefronts, >3,000 crimes, >3,000 vignettes** [verified, https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/santiago_david_procedurally_crafting_manhattan.pdf]. The pipeline is explicitly staged — **Roads/Alleys → Ground → Ground Modifiers → Tiles → Buildings → Ground Details → Hand Crafting** — where "Hand Crafting" is a *named, separate, later stage*: the "Buildings" slide is plain white massing boxes, and only after Ground Details and Hand Crafting does it become the lived-in Chinatown storefront row with painted signage. Rooftop water towers appear constantly across the deck's skyline shots — treated as a signature silhouette element, not incidental. (Shadows of Doubt's devblog #8, "Simulating a City," was read directly and covers only citizen simulation, not architecture — cited only for that limit [verified, https://colepowered.com/shadows-of-doubt-devblog-8-simulating-a-city/]. Devblog #21, "How Voxels Saved the Project," explains the voxel choice as a small-team production-speed decision, not an anti-repetition one [likely, https://colepowered.com/shadows-of-doubt-devblog-21-how-voxels-saved-the-project/] — a different point, covered by other agents' lanes.)

### 2.3 Controlled randomness

AI City already has the right *infrastructure*: `districts/plan.mjs` defines `noise(x,z,n)`, a per-coordinate hash producing a stable 0–1 value that every building, floor count and prop already derives from. **The mechanism is correct; the enum it feeds is too small.** `familyFor(i,j)` maps every lot to exactly one of 3 strings; `mixes.*` in `identity/borough-plan.mjs` are exactly 6 entries each, selected by `(row*3+k+shift)%6` — a deterministic cycle, not a shuffle. Widening these tables and switching cycle→shuffle costs no rendering budget, only data-table entries and a different selection function.

**Blue noise / Poisson-disc placement** is the standard fix for "grid of identical objects" and "empty flat plane": it guarantees minimum spacing between objects without a grid's visible alignment or naive random placement's clumping [likely, general CG technique, https://nothings.org/gamedev/blue_noise/]. Cheap (CPU-side, generation-time), and the right tool for parking-lot cars, dock cargo, and lawn clutter.

**Height jitter within zoning** has a real NYC precedent: the **1916 Zoning Resolution** required setbacks as buildings rose (five formulas by street width), producing "wedding cake" massing until 1961 [likely, https://buildingtheskyline.org/revisiting-1916-i/, https://thegreatestgrid.mcny.org/greatest-grid/manhattan-monumentality/294]. AI City's `corePlan` (`districts/landmarks-plan.mjs`) already produces 3-tier stepped massing — the fix isn't a new mechanic, it's making the tiers less uniform between towers.

**Lot-width variation** is the best-evidenced NYC fact here: a Bromley 1891 atlas block cited by the Gotham Center shows widths from **16 to 28 ft on one block**, "the mix of widths is typical" — not the exception [verified, https://www.gothamcenter.org/blog/notes-on-19th-century-lot-sizes]. Nominal width was 20–25 ft × 100 ft deep, but few actual rowhouse groups used a full uniform 25 ft; this variation is named as "one of the principal sources of the island's sense of human scale."

**Module-library sizing** has no single universal answer, but the Spider-Man ratio (~1 unique prefab per 2.5 buildings, AAA budget) is a real anchor, for a camera that spends most of its time *below* rooflines. AI City's default **overhead three-quarter view** is far less forgiving of footprint/roofline repetition than a street-level game — a repeated roofline reads instantly from above. That argues for **spending variation budget on roofline/footprint/massing first, ground-floor signage second** — Astra's current effort ratio is inverted (excellent storefronts, weak roofs).

### 2.4 NYC-specific rules for believable blocks

- **Lot widths**: nominal 20–25 ft × 100 ft deep, actual range ~16–31 ft on real blocks [verified, Gotham Center].
- **Walk-up vs. tenement vs. brownstone vs. loft**: *walk-up* = any building up to ~6 stories with no elevator, a functional category not a style. *Tenement* = purpose-built multi-family working-class housing (Jacob Riis-era, 5-story brick, minimal ornament, maximizing density). *Brownstone* = 3–5 story rowhouse faced in brown sandstone, built 1800–1925 for the middle class, raised stoop to an elevated parlor floor, carved lintels, cornice — "function drove the form" (light, air, distance from street noise) [likely, https://www.brownstoner.com/architecture/what-is-a-row-house-brooklyn-townhouse-architecture-history/, https://www.realdirect.com/blog/apartment-types/guide-to-new-york-city-building-types-2/]. *Loft* = later industrial-to-residential conversion: wide window bays, big floor plates, exposed structure — the visual odd one out.
- **Corner stores**: bodegas are "most famously" corner-sited, ~13,000 across NYC as of 2020 [likely, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodega]. Corner-only placement (never mid-block) is a cheap, correct rule.
- **Rooftop water tanks**: sources converge on **"buildings over six stories"** as the trigger — gravity-fed pressure from street mains runs out around 60 ft [likely, https://www.vitalcitynyc.org/nyc-wooden-water-tanks-architecture-history/, https://www.balkanplumbing.com/roof-top-water-tanks-nyc/]. Governing section: NYC Plumbing Code **§606.5.1**, "Water Pressure Booster and Gravity House Tank Systems" [likely — section identified, exact threshold text not directly readable (up.codes chapter 15 lacks it; amlegal.com returned HTTP 403)]. Construction, verified: cedar/redwood cooperage, 10–12 ft tall, ~10,000 gal, conical cap, 25–30 yr life [verified, Vital City]. **Confirms the brief's suspicion**: a 6-story walk-up sits at the threshold; a 3–4 story rowhouse or brownstone should not have one.
- **Fire escapes**: 1867 Act required them but went unenforced (by 1900, 98 of 2,877 sampled tenements had none, 653 rear-only). The 1879 "Old Law" required escapes plus a window per room, producing "dumbbell" airshaft massing. The 1901 "New Law" was the first actually enforced, upgrading ladders to fixed stair-and-balcony [verified, Village Preservation]. Rule of thumb: front ladder-style = pre-1901; balcony-and-stair = post-1901; brownstones generally carry none.
- **Storefront rhythm**: tied directly to lot width — one storefront per ~20–30 ft of frontage; it's the same variable as lot-width variation above.
- **Alleys**: rare in Manhattan. Where they exist, they were "created jointly by all adjoining lots" mid-19th-century for rear deliveries/utilities, **privately owned**, not city-maintained [likely, https://www.herrick.com/publications/the-alleys-of-new-york-2/]. AI City's empty docks/mall lots shouldn't be "fixed" with Manhattan-style alleys — that's not how mid-block service actually works there.
- **SoHo cast-iron / Brooklyn brownstone / Bronx walk-up**: SoHo-Cast Iron was LPC-designated in 1973 (~26 blocks, ~500 buildings; extended 2010), defined by mass-produced cast-iron facades with large windows and repeated classical ornament — the most *industrially uniform-looking* of the three, which is period-correct (cast iron was chosen historically because it was cheap to mass-produce identical ornament) [likely, NYPAP, Village Preservation]. Brooklyn brownstone blocks read as middle-class rowhouse: stoops, cornices, carved lintels, 3–5 stories [likely, Brownstoner]. **Bronx walk-up specifics weren't well covered by sources found here** — treat as weak/[anecdotal]: later-built (1900s–1920s), plainer brick multifamily along transit corridors, extending the tenement vocabulary rather than a distinct language. See open question §5.

### 2.5 Hero moments

No source gave a clean "X hero landmarks per district" rule — it appears to be a design choice each team makes, not a documented convention. The most useful benchmark is **AI City's own original map**, charming specifically *because* of its landmarks: a stepped-dome Hall of Justice with a statue plaza, a stepped-glass mall entrance, colour-blocked container stacks, a gantry crane — **roughly 4–6 total for the whole city**, each breaking the surrounding rule (different silhouette, material, height) rather than a bigger copy of nearby buildings. AI City's current `corePlan` (`districts/landmarks-plan.mjs`) already does real seed-driven landmark generation — 9 downtown parcels produce 11 towers, single or twin, cycling 3 material finishes with 3-tier stepped massing — but every one is still a *parametric* variation of the same shape family. At "one memorable building in the skyline" scale that reads as flat, because nothing in it is allowed to violate its own rules the way the original Hall of Justice did.

## 4. What not to do

- **Don't rewrite the generators toward a full Parish & Müller / CityEngine-style system.** Subversion is the cautionary case: a small team sank years into generator polish while the game on top of it stalled, and shelved the project in 2011 for it. AI City's per-lot seed + family/mix-table architecture is the right shape already; it needs wider tables and band-level noise, not a rewrite.
- **Don't fix repetition with more per-object randomness** (random rotation/scale/color on every window or house independently). Every technique here gets its "handmade" read from *local coherence* — neighbors agreeing — not from noise. Per-object randomness alone produces the "checker of beige window panels" look the brief already flagged as a tell.
- **Don't put a rooftop water tank on every building.** Reserve them for roughly six stories and up; a tank on a 3-story rowhouse is itself a tell.
- **Don't invent a Manhattan back-alley system** to fix empty industrial/dock/mall lots. Real Manhattan barely has alleys; the fix is anchor objects and blue-noise scatter, not carving lanes into superblocks.
- **Don't spread the landmark budget evenly** — a "hero" building per district or per parcel stops being a landmark. Every case study treats landmarks as rare, locally rule-breaking exceptions.
