# Stopping the hive mind: preventing lexical convergence in multi-agent LLM simulations

**Status: research, 2026-05-18.** Written before AI City for an earlier LLM social-feed simulation; its findings apply to any long-running
agent simulation. Papers are cited by name as the report gave them; figures are as those papers report them and were not re-checked for this page.


**Lexical convergence in multi-agent LLM systems is not a bug in any one architecture — it's a documented property of how transformers process shared context.** When agents read each other's raw text, the attention mechanism progressively down-weights the original persona prompt and up-weights recent context tokens, causing all agents to drift toward the most statistically salient voice in the shared feed. Research now confirms this convergence is measurable, asymmetric (structured voices dominate casual ones), and compounds with each cycle. The good news: a layered defense combining semantic abstraction, inference-time logit manipulation, and memory decontamination can arrest this drift almost entirely — and open-weight models give access to the most powerful mitigation tools available.

The pattern — one agent's technical "protocol/compile/algorithm" vocabulary spreading to every other agent — maps onto findings from multiple 2024–2025 papers that reveal why formal/technical language is inherently "stickier" in LLM generation. The solution stack involves interventions at five layers: what agents see (context isolation), how they attend (split-softmax), what tokens get generated (contrastive decoding), what gets stored (memory rewriting), and what gets published (style validation). No single technique suffices, but the combination is highly effective.

## The science of why agents merge

The most directly relevant paper is **"Emergent Convergence in Multi-Agent LLM Annotation"** (BlackboxNLP/ACL 2025), which simulated 7,500 multi-agent discussions generating 125,000+ utterances. The authors tracked lexical and semantic convergence using ROUGE scores and intrinsic dimensionality measures (TwoNN-Id). Their central finding: **LLM groups converge both lexically and semantically, developing asymmetric influence patterns** where certain agents disproportionately shape group language — even without explicit role prompting.

Perez et al.'s **"When LLMs Play the Telephone Game"** (ICLR 2025, Inria) applied cultural evolution transmission chain methodology to LLMs and found that iterative agent-to-agent text transmission creates **attractor states** — stable linguistic patterns that text converges toward regardless of starting conditions. More open-ended instructions produce stronger attraction effects. The convergence direction is determined by the model's training distribution, which is overwhelmingly formal, edited prose.

The mechanistic explanation comes from Harvard's **"Measuring and Controlling Persona Drift"** (Li et al., COLM 2024), which demonstrated that in LLaMA2-chat-70B, significant persona drift occurs **within 8 conversation rounds**. The root cause is attention decay: as the context window fills with tokens from other agents, softmax attention on the original system prompt tokens drops sharply between turns. The agent doesn't just lose its voice — it actively adopts the most salient voice in its context window. A run of 20+ cycles far exceeds this threshold.

## Why "protocol" beats "vibes" every time

The dominance of structured/technical vocabulary over casual language is not accidental — it's baked into transformer architecture at multiple levels. Lai et al.'s **"Style-Specific Neurons"** (EMNLP 2024) found a striking asymmetry: transferring from impolite to polite style achieves **~80% accuracy**, while the reverse direction achieves only **~12%**. LLM training corpora — dominated by Wikipedia, academic papers, books, and news — encode far more neurons for formal registers than casual ones.

This asymmetry compounds through three additional mechanisms. First, **in-context learning over-indexes on structural patterns**: a 2025 Microsoft/University of York study found that ICL "over-focuses on spurious statistical features within the prompt," meaning the LLM treats formatting patterns and technical vocabulary as task-relevant signals to reproduce. Second, **RLHF amplifies formality**: instruction tuning improves lexical diversity but constrains syntactic and semantic diversity (Guo et al., TACL 2024), creating a stronger gravitational pull toward structured output. Third, **technical terms have sharper probability distributions** — words like "protocol" appear in highly consistent contexts across training data, giving them higher conditional probability than slang terms with diffuse usage patterns.

Frisch & Giulianelli's **"LLM Agents in Interaction"** (ACL Workshop 2024) confirmed this directly in a multi-agent setting: GPT-3.5 agents conditioned on different Big Five personality profiles show **asymmetric linguistic alignment**, with some personality types being inherently more "contagious" than others. Stronger, more structured personas dominate — matching the observed dominance of the most technical voice.

## The semantic firewall: abstracting meaning from surface form

The single highest-impact intervention is **never exposing agents to each other's raw text**. Research on content-style disentanglement provides multiple approaches to build this intermediary layer.

The simplest practical method comes from the **Delete-Retrieve-Generate** paradigm (Li et al., NAACL 2018): identify style-marker tokens, delete them, then regenerate fluent text from the remaining content skeleton. For a social feed, only the delete-and-regenerate steps are needed — strip distinctive vocabulary markers and produce a neutral summary. This can be implemented as a single LLM call: *"Summarize the following post in neutral, factual language. Preserve all semantic content but remove all stylistic markers, slang, and distinctive vocabulary."*

A more robust approach uses **structured intermediary representations**. Instead of passing text between agents, extract key claims as structured data — `{topic: "build_performance", stance: "frustrated", claim: "compilation_too_slow"}` — and let each agent reconstruct the meaning in their own voice from this schema. Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) parsing offers the most thorough style stripping but adds complexity. In practice, a JSON extraction step provides 80% of the benefit at 20% of the complexity.

Tone-only notifications for replies are a step in this direction. The research suggests extending the pattern: **comments should also pass through the abstraction layer** before entering another agent's context. Even partial exposure to raw text creates contamination vectors — the attention mechanism will latch onto distinctive tokens regardless of how small the exposure window is.

## Memory decontamination: rewriting the past in the agent's own voice

The Generative Agents architecture (Park et al., 2023) stores observations in **whatever language they were perceived in** — there is no style normalization. When Agent A observes Agent B's post, Agent B's vocabulary enters Agent A's memory stream verbatim, creating a persistent contamination source that re-enters the context window during memory retrieval across all future cycles.

The fix is a **memory rewriting pipeline** inserted between observation and storage. The Reflective Memory Management framework (Tan et al., ACL 2025) provides the architectural pattern: a "Prospective Reflection" step dynamically summarizes interactions into a personalized memory bank. This is exactly where style decontamination should occur. The implementation is straightforward:

1. **Extract** semantic content from the observation as structured data: `{event: "post_comment", topic: "compile_times", agent: "agent_b", sentiment: "frustrated"}`
2. **Reconstruct** the memory in the agent's own voice via a dedicated prompt: *"Rewrite this event in YOUR voice, using only YOUR vocabulary: [structured data + agent persona]"*
3. **Store** only the reconstructed version — never the raw observation

The **MaRS framework** (2025) offers a complementary approach through its Reflection-Summary consolidation policy, which periodically summarizes and compresses memories. This natural compression step strips surface-level vocabulary while retaining semantic content. A hybrid policy — staging temporal heuristics into importance-aware methods into reflective consolidation — delivers the best composite performance. Schedule memory consolidation every N cycles with an explicit style re-grounding prompt: *"Rewrite the following memories using ONLY your characteristic vocabulary and speech patterns."*

## Inference-time interventions: manipulating logits on open-weight models

With open weights, the most powerful class of solutions is available — direct logit manipulation during generation. Four techniques are particularly applicable, listed in order of implementation complexity.

**Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) with negative prompting** is the most immediately deployable. Adapted from image diffusion models by Sanchez et al. (2023), CFG for text computes: `logits_final = (1 + γ) × logits_with_persona - γ × logits_with_negative_condition`. Set the negative condition to other agents' recent text or vocabulary fingerprint, and the model actively steers away from that style. The γ parameter controls steering strength. This is **already implemented in HuggingFace Transformers** (`UnbatchedClassifierFreeGuidanceLogitsProcessor`), llama.cpp, and SillyTavern. Cost: 2× forward passes per token.

**DExperts-style contrastive decoding** (Liu et al., ACL 2021) combines base model logits with expert and anti-expert signals: `logits_final = logits_base + α × (logits_expert - logits_anti_expert)`. The anti-expert can be the same model prompted with other agents' vocabulary patterns. Tokens only get high probability if the expert (agent's own voice) favors them AND the anti-expert (other agents' voices) disfavors them. Code is available at `github.com/alisawuffles/DExperts`.

**The Antislop Sampler** (Paech et al., ICLR 2026) offers **sequence-level backtracking suppression** — a significant advance over token-level logit bias. When a banned phrase is detected during generation, the sampler backtracks to the first token of the banned sequence, reduces its probability by `p_new = p_old × 10^(-10s)`, and resamples. This operates at the string/phrase level with no collateral damage on unrelated tokens sharing subword prefixes. It supports soft bans (configurable ban-strength 0–1), can handle 8,000+ suppression patterns simultaneously, and supports regex matching. The throughput cost is **69–96% reduction** depending on banlist size, which a simulation of a few agents can absorb. Build per-agent banlists dynamically from vocabulary characteristic of other agents.

**Activation steering via Representation Engineering** (Zou et al., 2023) extracts "style vectors" by running contrasting inputs through the model and computing activation differences at target layers. During generation, add the target agent's style vector and subtract other agents' style vectors. The **PERSONA framework** (2025) extends this with algebraic composition of orthogonal personality trait vectors, achieving **91% win rates** on personality adaptation benchmarks — competitive with supervised fine-tuning. This requires no gradient updates and adds minimal inference overhead (just vector addition at target layers). The caveat from "What Can We Actually Steer?" (2025): style cues are "highly steerable," but interference between multiple steering vectors can degrade coherence.

## What the major simulation frameworks get wrong

A survey of the six most prominent multi-agent simulation frameworks reveals a consistent blind spot: **none explicitly addresses linguistic convergence between agents**.

**Generative Agents** (Park et al., 2023) relies entirely on initial character descriptions and memory retrieval for personality persistence. There is no mechanism to prevent stylistic homogenization through the shared environment. The **ID-RAG paper** (2025) explicitly diagnoses this failure: *"current generative agent frameworks lack persistent persona coherence,"* leading to *"identity drift, ignoring established beliefs, and the propagation of hallucinations in multi-agent systems."*

**CAMEL** (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023) acknowledges "conversation deviation" and "role flipping" as failure modes, addressing them through format enforcement ("Always end YOUR_SOLUTION with: Next request") rather than linguistic style preservation. **AgentVerse** (Chen et al., 2023) focuses on task performance, not voice preservation. **Concordia** (Google DeepMind, 2023) grounds identity via formative memories and a "Three Key Questions" model but has no style isolation mechanism — Concordia v2.0 added "converging vs. diverging" dialogue balance, but this targets topic diversity, not voice distinctiveness.

**Affordable Generative Agents** (Yu et al., TMLR 2024) compresses dialogue information for cost reduction, which paradoxically risks accelerating convergence by stripping the distinctive features that differentiate agents. The most promising framework is **SALM** (2025), which provides **formal mathematical bounds on personality stability** across 4,000+ timesteps — but it operationalizes personality as a behavioral vector, not a linguistic fingerprint.

The **Moltbook analysis** (2026) of 129,773+ AI agents on a social network offers a critical insight: despite massive scale, agents *"converge toward homogenized affirmation"* with *"repetitive lexical and topical patterns."* The researchers conclude: **"Architected diversity, which assigns agents demographic or attitudinal labels, is insufficient. Without mechanisms that operationalize worldview divergence, epistemic friction, or genuine disagreement, multi-agent systems default to statistical centrality."** Persona descriptions alone cannot prevent convergence; active architectural enforcement is needed.

## The recommended defense stack

Based on the full body of research, here is the layered architecture ordered by implementation priority and expected impact:

- **Layer 1 — Context isolation with semantic abstraction** (highest impact, easy): Never expose agents to each other's raw posts. Route all cross-agent content through a paraphraser that extracts meaning as structured JSON or neutral summaries. This single intervention addresses the primary contamination vector.
- **Layer 2 — Style exemplar anchoring** (high impact, trivial): Include 3–5 canonical writing samples in each agent's prompt at every generation call. Research shows few-shot exemplars achieve **23× higher style-matching accuracy** than text descriptions of style. This is far more effective than describing the persona.
- **Layer 3 — Memory rewriting pipeline** (high impact, moderate): Before storing any observation referencing other agents' content, extract structured semantics and reconstruct the memory in the agent's own voice. Schedule periodic memory consolidation with explicit style re-grounding.
- **Layer 4 — CFG or DExperts contrastive decoding** (high impact, moderate): Use other agents' vocabulary fingerprints as the negative condition in classifier-free guidance, or as the anti-expert in DExperts. This actively pushes token probabilities away from contaminated distributions at generation time.
- **Layer 5 — Style validator with reject/regenerate** (medium impact, moderate): After each generation, run a lightweight persona adherence check (following TinyTroupe's Proposition pattern). If the output contains vocabulary characteristic of other agents, reject and regenerate. Microsoft's TinyTroupe framework provides working code for this pattern at `github.com/microsoft/TinyTroupe`.

For advanced optimization, three additional techniques are worth exploring: **split-softmax** (Li et al., `github.com/likenneth/persona_drift`) guarantees minimum attention to persona tokens regardless of context length; **per-agent LoRA adapters** (following the Neeko framework, `github.com/weiyifan1023/Neeko`) create parametric style bias that's harder to override than prompt-based approaches; and **PERSONA activation vectors** (`arxiv:2602.15669`) enable training-free personality control through algebraic operations on extracted trait vectors.

## Conclusion

The vocabulary convergence problem in multi-agent LLM simulations stems from three interacting mechanisms: attention decay on persona prompts, asymmetric stylistic influence favoring formal/structured language, and memory contamination through stored raw observations. The research is unambiguous that no single technique — whether prompt engineering, logit manipulation, or architectural design — is sufficient in isolation. The formal/casual asymmetry is particularly stubborn because it's embedded in training data distributions, RLHF reward signals, and the statistical mechanics of in-context learning simultaneously.

The key insight: **the shared feed is the attack surface**. Every raw token from one agent that enters another agent's context window is a contamination vector, and the contamination compounds geometrically across cycles. The semantic abstraction layer — converting posts to structured meaning representations before cross-agent consumption — eliminates this vector at the source. Combined with inference-time contrastive decoding (available today for open-weight models through existing HuggingFace CFG processors) and memory rewriting, this creates a defense-in-depth architecture where convergence pressure is intercepted at input, generation, and storage. The Moltbook research's warning deserves emphasis: merely labeling agents with distinct personas is provably insufficient — active enforcement has to be built into the information flow itself.
